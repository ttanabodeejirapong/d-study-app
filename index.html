Slide 1 • p.16–21

Economics, Economic Agents, and the Big Questions

Economics studies the choices of individuals, businesses, governments, and societies as they cope with scarcity and respond to incentives, coordinating resources to achieve maximum output or their objectives.

Decision framework used by the professor

  • Consumer / Buyer: scarce resource = income or money → chooses what to buy/consume → objective = maximize satisfaction / utility.
  • Firm / Seller: scarce resource = budget → chooses what to produce and sell → objective = maximize profit.
  • Government: uses policies and budget → objective = maximize social welfare.

Two big economic questions

  • How do choices determine WHAT, HOW, and FOR WHOM goods and services are produced?
  • When do choices made in self-interest also promote social interest?

Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

  • Microeconomics: individual consumers, firms, individual markets, and how government influences their choices.
  • Macroeconomics: aggregate / total effects on the national or global economy, such as GDP, inflation, and employment.
ช่วยจำ: Micro ไม่ได้แปลว่า “เรื่องเล็ก” แต่ดู individual decision / individual market; Macro ดูภาพรวมแบบ aggregate.
Slide 2 • p.22–24

The Economic Way of Thinking

Positive vs Normative Statements

  • Positive statement = WHAT IS — a testable description or explanation of how the economic world works.
  • Normative statement = WHAT OUGHT TO BE — a judgment about what should be done or which solution is best under stated conditions/reasons.

Ceteris Paribus

Change ONE factor at a time and hold ALL other relevant factors constant to isolate cause and effect.
Ceteris paribus = “other things being equal” → ใช้ตัดผลของปัจจัยอื่นออก เพื่อดูผลของตัวแปรที่เราสนใจให้ชัด.
Slide 3 • p.25–33

The Economic Problem: Scarcity, Market, and Production Choices

Scarcity = available resources are insufficient to satisfy all wants → choices must be made → choices depend on incentives.
  • A resource can be scarce even if it is free: the test is whether everyone can obtain as much as they want.
  • The professor links scarcity to efficient resource allocation: because we cannot satisfy every want, the economy needs a way to decide which uses of resources receive priority.
  • Prices and markets help coordinate those choices by connecting what buyers are willing to obtain with what sellers are willing to produce.

Market — what the word means here

A market is a broad arrangement through which buyers and sellers interact and transact for a price. It does not require everyone to stand in one physical place: the key idea is the interaction between buyers and sellers.

Producer decision 1 — WHAT should be produced?

The lecture lists four categories. The useful exam skill is to classify the item by who ultimately uses it / where it is sold, not by whether it is physically large or expensive.

Consumption goods & servicesGoods or services produced for consumption. A household service such as a haircut is a service, and when a household buys it for personal use it belongs on the consumption side.
Capital goodsProduced items used to help produce other goods and services. A machine used by a factory is capital because the machine itself was produced and is now used in production.
Government goods & servicesGoods and services produced/purchased for government activity or public provision. The important distinction is the government sector's role, not the physical form of the item.
Export goods & servicesDomestic production sold to buyers abroad. Application example: rice produced domestically and sold overseas is classified as an export good.
Application-note: the lecture itself lists these four categories. Examples such as “factory machine” or “rice sold abroad” are classification applications used to make the categories testable, not quotations from the slide.

Producer decision 2 — HOW should production be organized?

HOW means choosing and combining the inputs used in production. The professor groups those inputs into four factors of production:

Land“Gifts of nature” — natural resources used in production. It is broader than a plot of land.
LaborHuman time and effort devoted to producing goods and services, whether workers are skilled or unskilled.
CapitalProduced productive resources: tools, instruments, machines, buildings, and other produced items used to make goods/services. In this lecture, capital does not simply mean cash.
EntrepreneurshipThe human resource that organizes land, labor, and capital and runs the business decision process.

Producer decision 3 — FOR WHOM, and why “Paid Incomes” appear

Production uses resources owned by somebody. When a firm uses those resources, the owners receive payments. That is why the professor connects the production side to Paid Incomes:

Land → Rent   |   Labor → Wages   |   Capital → Interest   |   Entrepreneurship → Profit/Loss
RentIncome paid for the use of land / natural resources.
WagesIncome paid for the services of labor.
InterestIncome paid for the use of capital in the factor-income framework used on the slide.
Profit / LossThe entrepreneur's residual income outcome from running the business and organizing production.
Two viewpoints, same flow: for the factor owner, rent/wages/interest/profit are income received. For the firm, payments made to use those resources are part of the cost of production. This is the logic behind the professor's handwritten “Price → Cost of production → Paid incomes” flow. Do not read it as a literal accounting identity saying every product's sticker price must equal one single factor payment.
คิดแบบง่าย ๆ: โรงงานต้อง “เช่า/จ่าย” ทรัพยากรเพื่อผลิตสินค้า → เงินที่โรงงานจ่ายเป็น cost แต่พอมองจากคนที่เป็นเจ้าของทรัพยากร เงินก้อนเดียวกันคือ income. เช่น firm จ่ายค่าแรง = cost ของ firm แต่ = wage income ของ worker.
Slide 4 • p.34–39

Opportunity Cost and Economic vs Accounting Concepts

Opportunity cost = the value of the next-best alternative forgone.
  • It is not the sum of every alternative you did not choose.
  • It does not need to be an actual cash payment.
  • The lecture connects opportunity cost to implicit cost, which is generally not shown as a separate business record.

Cost concepts

Accounting Cost = Explicit Cost
Economic Cost = Explicit Cost + Implicit Cost

Profit concepts

Accounting Profit = Revenue − Explicit Cost
Economic Profit = Revenue − Explicit Cost − Implicit Cost

Example logic from the lecture: if the owner invests money that could earn a return elsewhere, that forgone return is an implicit/opportunity cost.

คำยาก: forgone = สิ่งที่ยอมเสียไปเพราะเราเลือกอีกทางหนึ่ง. Implicit = มีต้นทุนจริงทางเศรษฐศาสตร์ แต่ไม่ได้เห็นเป็นรายการจ่ายเงินแยกชัด ๆ.
Slide 5 • p.40–42

Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF): Core Ideas

PPF = the boundary between combinations that can and cannot be produced, given available factors of production and the current state of technology.

The three features introduced by p.42

  • Attainable vs Unattainable: the PPF shows the production limit.
  • Efficient vs Inefficient: efficient means one more unit cannot be produced without producing less of something else; inefficient means more can be produced without giving something up.
  • Tradeoff vs Free Lunch: a tradeoff requires giving something up; a free lunch means getting more without giving something up.

Bridge into the market model on p.42

Buyers → Demand   |   Sellers / resource owners → Supply   |   Market interaction → Equilibrium.
At p.42: these three ideas have been introduced. The detailed graph applications and student-PPF examples begin in the next summary (p.43 onward).

Slide 1 • p.43–49

PPF Applications: Reading the Graph

1. Attainable vs Unattainable

  • On or inside the PPF → attainable.
  • Outside the PPF → unattainable with current resources and technology.

2. Efficient vs Inefficient

  • On the PPF → efficient production: resources are fully used.
  • Inside the PPF → inefficient: some resources are unused or misallocated, so more of one or both goods may be produced without sacrifice.

3. Tradeoffs vs Free Lunches

  • Movement along the PPF → tradeoff.
  • Movement from an inside point toward the frontier can be a free lunch because unused resources are brought into use.

Your personal PPF

The lecture applies the same logic to a student choosing between GPA and time for leisure / work. A higher GPA requires giving up leisure or income; the forgone leisure/income is the opportunity cost.

ช่วยอ่านกราฟ: จุด “inside” ไม่ได้แปลว่าทำไม่ได้ — ทำได้แต่ inefficient. จุด “outside” ต่างหากที่ยังทำไม่ได้ภายใต้ข้อจำกัดปัจจุบัน.
Slide 2 • p.50–58

Demand: Buyer Behavior

Supply–demand analysis is introduced as a fundamental tool for studying how changing conditions affect market price and production.

Quantity Demanded and Law of Demand

Quantity demanded (Qd) = the amount buyers are willing to purchase at a specific time.
Qd = Qd(P)   →   other things equal, P ↑ → Qd ↓ and P ↓ → Qd ↑.

This negative relationship gives the demand curve its downward slope.

Determinants of Demand — beyond the good's own price

  • Income: income ↑ → demand ↑ for a normal good; income ↑ → demand ↓ for an inferior good.
  • Prices of related goods: for substitutes, price of one ↑ → demand for the other ↑. For complements, price of one ↑ → demand for the other ↓.
  • Tastes: stronger preference → demand ↑.
  • Expectations: if consumers expect the good's future price to rise, current demand rises in the professor's example.
  • Number of buyers: more buyers → market demand ↑.

Movement Along vs Shift of Demand

Change in the good's own price → move along the current demand curve.
Change in another determinant → the entire demand curve shifts.
  • Rightward shift → greater quantity demanded at every price.
  • Leftward shift → smaller quantity demanded at every price.

Lecture example: greater use of EVs reduces demand for oil used by ICE vehicles → oil demand shifts left.

จุดที่ข้อสอบชอบหลอก: own price เปลี่ยน = movement along; income/taste/related-good price/expectation/#buyers เปลี่ยน = shift.
Slide 3 • p.59–64

Supply: Seller Behavior

Quantity Supplied and Law of Supply

Quantity supplied (Qs) = the amount sellers are willing to sell in the market at a specific time.
Qs = Qs(P)   →   other things equal, P ↑ → Qs ↑ and P ↓ → Qs ↓.

This positive relationship gives the supply curve its upward slope.

Determinants of Supply

  • Input prices: input cost ↑ → supply ↓; lower production costs can shift supply right.
  • Technology / innovation: more efficient production → supply ↑.
  • Expectations: if sellers expect their product's future price to be higher, the annotation shows they may wait to sell → current supply ↓.
  • Number of sellers: more sellers → market supply ↑.
  • The professor's annotation also lists price of related goods among non-price supply factors.

Movement Along vs Shift of Supply

Change in the good's own price → move along the current supply curve.
Change in another determinant → shift to a new supply curve.

If production costs fall, firms can supply the same quantity at a lower price or a larger quantity at the same price → supply shifts right.

จำเหมือน Demand: ถ้าเปลี่ยน “ราคาของสินค้านั้นเอง” ให้คิด movement along ก่อน; ถ้าเป็นต้นทุน เทคโนโลยี expectation จำนวนผู้ขาย ฯลฯ ให้คิด shift.
Slide 4 • p.65–70

Market Equilibrium, Surplus, and Shortage

Market equilibrium: Qd = Qs. The corresponding price is the equilibrium / market-clearing price (Pe), and the quantity is the equilibrium quantity (Qe).

If price is not at equilibrium

  • P > Pe: Qs > Qd → excess supply / surplus → market pressure pushes price down toward Pe.
  • P < Pe: Qd > Qs → excess demand / shortage → market pressure pushes price up toward Pe.

The lecture calls equilibrium a stable condition when demand and supply curves are unchanged: if market price moves away from Pe, the market mechanism tends to move it back.

Can equilibrium change?

Yes. A shift in demand and/or supply changes the equilibrium point.

Lecture example: war/tension lowers oil production → Supply shifts left → equilibrium price rises and equilibrium quantity falls.
คำยาก: market-clearing = ราคาที่ทำให้ของที่คนอยากซื้อเท่ากับของที่คนอยากขาย; surplus = ของเหลือ; shortage = ของขาด.

Lecture 2.1 • Part 1 • p.7–12

GDP Definition, Scope, and What Counts

GDP = the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given time period.

The professor’s 4-part reading of the definition

  • Market value → VALUE produced. For current-period nominal GDP, add price × quantity across final products.
  • Final goods and services → WHAT produced. Intermediate goods are excluded to avoid counting the same production more than once. The lecture’s logic is: count the final value, or equivalently sum the value added at each stage, so intermediate production is not double counted.
  • Within a country → WHERE produced. GDP follows the location of production, not the nationality of the owner.
  • Given time period → WHEN produced, such as a quarter or year.

High-yield inclusion rules from annotations

  • Stocks and bonds: financial products → the purchase itself is not current production, so it is not counted as GDP.
  • New home: counted as Investment (I), not household Consumption.
  • Software / firm purchases: classification depends on how the item is used. The lecture annotation contrasts short-lived intermediate use with longer-lived capital/investment use.
  • GDP vs GNP idea: GDP = production inside the country; GNP-type logic follows nationality/residency of productive resources.
จำแบบข้อสอบ: GDP definition แยกเป็น VALUE / WHAT / WHERE / WHEN. คำว่า domestic ให้ดู “ผลิตที่ไหน” ไม่ใช่ “เจ้าของสัญชาติอะไร”.
Exam trap: “Final” does not simply mean “bought by a person.” A good bought by a firm can still be capital investment if it is a final capital good.
Lecture 2.1 • Part 2 • p.13–23

Measuring GDP: Expenditure Approach & Circular Flow

The lecture moves away from identifying every final/intermediate transaction one-by-one and uses alternative approaches that must measure the same economy.

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

Four expenditure sectors

  • C — Consumption: household spending on goods and services, except new housing.
  • I — Investment: capital equipment, inventories, structures, and new housing. Inventory is current production that has not yet been sold to a final user.
  • G — Government purchases: government spending on currently produced goods and services.
  • NX = X − M — Net exports: exports minus imports.

Why transfer payments are not G

The annotation uses free cash/subsidy-type transfers to show that a transfer is not payment for a currently produced good or service. If the household later spends the money, that final purchase is recorded where the actual final expenditure occurs. Counting the transfer itself again would create a double-counting problem.

Circular-flow logic

  • Households, firms, government, and the international sector interact through goods/services and factor markets.
  • For the whole economy: Expenditure = Income, because every buyer’s spending is a seller’s income.
จุดหลอก: ซื้อบ้านใหม่ = I, เงินแจก/transfer ≠ G, imports ต้องถูกหักใน NX เพราะ C/I/G อาจรวมการใช้จ่ายสินค้านำเข้าไว้แล้ว.
Lecture 2.1 • Part 3 • p.24–30

Income Approach + Nominal vs Real GDP

Income approach

GDP can also be measured from the income paid for factors of production. The lecture links this back to the factor-payment framework.

Factor incomes: Labor → Wages • Land → Rent • Capital → Interest • Entrepreneurship → Profit
  • Compensation of employees covers labor compensation, including more than take-home pay.
  • Net operating surplus collects other factor incomes such as interest, rent, profits, and proprietors’ income.
  • The annotated income calculation then makes market adjustments, including items such as indirect taxes net of subsidies and depreciation, so the income-side measure aligns with GDP at market value.

Nominal GDP vs Real GDP

Nominal GDP uses current-year prices × current-year quantities.
Real GDP holds prices at a base-year level and uses current quantities, so changes mainly reflect production rather than price changes.

The professor explicitly asks whether a higher GDP number always means greater prosperity. No. If nominal GDP rises because prices rise while physical output falls, the economy has not necessarily produced more.

Base year: Nominal GDP = Real GDP in the base year because both use the same year’s prices and quantities.
จำ: Nominal มีทั้ง P และ Q เปลี่ยนได้; Real ล็อก P ที่ base year เพื่อดู Q/production ให้ชัด.
Lecture 2.1 • Part 4 • p.31–42

Using Real GDP: Living Standard, Per Capita, and PPP

Comparing over time

Real GDP is useful for tracking real production. For an individual-level standard-of-living comparison, the lecture shifts attention to real GDP per person / GDP per capita so country size does not dominate the comparison.

GDP per capita = GDP ÷ Population

Comparing across countries

  • Converting local GDP per person with a market exchange rate does not automatically adjust for different price levels.
  • The lecture introduces Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) to account for purchasing-power differences.
  • A lower dollar-converted spending/GDP-per-capita number does not automatically mean a lower living standard when the local price level is also lower.

What GDP can and cannot tell you

The lecture treats GDP as a major macroeconomic indicator, not a complete definition of prosperity or well-being. Always distinguish higher money value from higher real output, and distinguish aggregate GDP from per-person living-standard comparisons.

Specific limitations listed in the lecture

  • Household production — useful non-market production can be omitted.
  • Underground economic activity — production can exist without being captured in official GDP.
  • Health and life expectancy — important for quality of life but not directly measured by GDP.
  • Leisure time — more output is not automatically more leisure/well-being.
  • Environmental quality — GDP can rise while environmental conditions worsen.
  • Political freedom and social justice — dimensions of well-being outside the GDP measure.
ข้อสอบตีความ: “ประเทศ A GDP ใหญ่กว่า B” ยังสรุปไม่ได้ว่าคน A อยู่ดีกว่า ต้องคิดอย่างน้อยเรื่อง population และ price level/PPP.

Lecture 2.2 • Part 1 • p.3–13

Employment, Unemployment, and Labor-Market Indicators

The opening annotation connects macro performance to the labor market: GDP and employment tend to move together, while unemployment tends to move in the opposite direction.

Classification rules used in the lecture

  • Employed: paid work for at least 1 hour, or unpaid work for at least 15 hours; also includes someone who did not work but still had a job from which they were temporarily laid off.
  • Unemployed: no employment + available for work + either actively applied for a job within the last four weeks or is waiting to be recalled after layoff.
  • Discouraged worker: has not made an effort to find a job in the last four weeks → not counted as unemployed and not in the labor force.
  • Full-time worker: works 35+ hours per week in the lecture’s worker-classification note.
  • Part-time worker: can be involuntary (wants full-time work but works part-time for economic reasons) or voluntary.
  • Aggregate hours: total hours worked by all workers over the period — a quantity-of-labor measure later reused in the Economic Growth lecture.

Why unemployment is a problem

  • Lost income and production.
  • Lost human capital as skills and productive capacity can deteriorate during unemployment.

Three labor-market indicators

Employment-to-Population Ratio = Employed ÷ Working-age Population × 100
Unemployment Rate = Unemployed ÷ Labor Force × 100
Labor Force Participation Rate = Labor Force ÷ Working-age Population × 100

The annotated slide labels higher employment-to-population and participation as generally better monitoring signals, while a lower unemployment rate is generally better.

Denominator trap: unemployment rate uses labor force, not population and not working-age population.
Lecture 2.2 • Part 2 • p.14–15

Sources and Types of Unemployment

How unemployment starts / ends

  • Sources: job losers, job leavers, entrants, and reentrants.
  • Ending unemployment: hires, recalls, or withdrawals from the labor force.

Four types

  • Frictional: normal labor turnover; people entering/leaving jobs and ongoing job creation/destruction.
  • Structural: technology or international competition changes the structure of jobs; skills/location no longer match available work. Annotation: shift from labor-intensive toward capital/technology-intensive industries.
  • Seasonal: employment changes with seasonal patterns, e.g. construction/farming.
  • Cyclical: changes with the business cycle; rises in recessions and falls in expansions.

The professor’s annotation stresses why classification matters: government support should be appropriate to the reason for unemployment.

แยกให้ได้: เปลี่ยนงานปกติ = frictional; skill mismatch จาก tech/competition = structural; ฤดูกาล = seasonal; recession/expansion = cyclical.
Lecture 2.2 • Part 3 • p.16–24

CPI, Inflation, Headline/Core, and How to Calculate

Inflation = a rise in the economy’s overall price level. Inflation rate = percentage change in the price level from the previous period.

CPI definition

CPI is a weighted average of prices paid by urban consumers for a fixed market basket of consumption goods and services. It is used to monitor changes in the cost of living over time.

CPIₜ = Cost of base-year basket at current-period prices ÷ Cost of the same basket at base-period prices × 100
Inflation rate = (CPIₜ − CPIₜ₋₁) ÷ CPIₜ₋₁ × 100

Construction logic from annotations

  • Select a market basket and keep quantities fixed to the base year.
  • Survey prices of the same / identical product types through time.
  • Compute the index, then compare current CPI with the previous period to obtain inflation.

Headline vs Core Inflation

  • Headline: includes the broad consumption basket and better reflects the private sector’s experienced cost of living.
  • Core: excludes raw food and energy; the annotation links it to government/monetary-policy monitoring because those prices are difficult to control and highly volatile.
Lecture 2.2 • Part 4 • p.25–35

CPI ≠ Exact Cost of Living: Biases and GDP Deflator

Why CPI is not a perfect cost-of-living measure

The lecture defines cost of living as the money needed to achieve a certain standard of living, then warns that CPI does not capture every component and relies on a fixed basket / identical-product assumptions.

Four CPI biases highlighted in the lecture

  • New-goods bias: new products can deliver more value/quality but may be absent from the fixed basket at first.
  • Quality-change bias: a higher price caused by better quality is not pure inflation, but CPI can count too much of it as a price increase.
  • Commodity-substitution bias: when one product becomes relatively expensive, consumers substitute toward cheaper alternatives, but a fixed basket does not fully capture that response.
  • Outlet-substitution bias: consumers may switch toward discount stores, while the survey may not fully capture the change in where people buy.

The slide notes an estimated overall tendency for CPI bias to overstate inflation.

GDP Deflator as an alternative price-level measure

GDP Deflator = Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP × 100
  • GDP deflator covers prices of all goods and services included in GDP.
  • CPI covers consumption goods and services and uses past/base-basket quantities.
  • The lecture notes that, in theory, the GDP deflator avoids several fixed-basket CPI biases, but in practice CPI remains widely used because it is direct and understandable for cost of living, and some deflator construction still depends on price indexes.
จำ contrast: CPI = consumer basket / fixed weights; GDP deflator = domestic GDP basket / changing production mix.

Lecture 3 • Concept • p.3–12

Economic Growth: Definition, Measurement, and Why Per Person Matters

Economic growth = sustained expansion of the PPF, measured as an increase in Real GDP (RGDP).
Economic growth rate = percentage change in Real GDP.

Real GDP per person

  • Used to focus on the individual standard-of-living side rather than only the size of the whole economy.
  • Real GDP per person grows only when Real GDP grows faster than population.
  • The lecture uses real GDP per person in country-income classification and as an indicator of economic-development outcomes.
  • Thailand is discussed in the context of the middle-income trap: moving to a higher income group requires a growth model capable of raising real GDP per person persistently.

Rule of 70

Years to double ≈ 70 ÷ annual percentage growth rate

The professor uses this rule to connect a target income level with the growth rate required to reach it over time.

Lecture 3 • Drivers • p.13–18

What Determines Real GDP Growth?

Real GDP = Aggregate Hours × Labor Productivity

The lecture organizes all influences on RGDP growth through two channels:

  1. Quantity of labor / Aggregate hours
  2. Labor productivity

Quantity of labor

  • Can rise through higher average hours worked.
  • Can rise when the labor force grows.
  • Labor force growth depends on the labor-force participation rate and population.
  • Participation and hours have practical upper limits; the lecture identifies population growth as the only sustainable source of continued quantity-of-labor growth.
  • But population growth alone does not guarantee higher RGDP per person; labor must also become more productive.

Labor productivity

Labor Productivity = Real GDP ÷ Aggregate Hours
  • Physical capital per worker: more/better tools, machines, buildings and productive capital raise output per hour.
  • Human capital: education, training and job experience raise worker productivity.
  • New technology: discoveries and advanced production methods raise productivity; the lecture stresses that physical capital often must increase for technology to be used effectively.
Professor’s bridge: to explain growth, do not stop at “more workers.” Ask whether productivity rises enough for Real GDP per person to increase.
Lecture 3 • Theory 1 • p.19–20

Classical Growth Theory — Smith & Malthus

Key condition in the lecture: exploding population + limited resources → long-run pressure that ends growth in RGDP per person.

Causal story

  • Technology advances and capital accumulation can initially raise productivity and RGDP per person.
  • Prosperity then encourages population growth.
  • More population reduces the amount of capital/resources available per worker.
  • Productivity and RGDP per person fall back toward a low level; technological change cannot permanently escape the population/resource pressure in this theory.

Professor’s evaluation

The lecture treats the historical condition as crucial: population explosion was a major concern when the theory was developed. Its limitation for current advanced economies is that population does not behave as the theory predicted.

แก่น Classical: productivity ↑ → prosperity ↑ → population ↑มาก → capital/resource per worker ↓ → per-person gain ถูกกินกลับ.
Lecture 3 • Theory 2 • p.21–22

Neoclassical Growth Theory — Solow

RGDP per person can keep increasing as long as technology keeps advancing.
  • The lecture presents population growth and technological change as determinants of growth that are not themselves explained by the RGDP growth rate.
  • Predicted RGDP growth ≈ population growth + labor-productivity growth.
  • As technology advances, firms invest and expand to exploit it; saving/investment raise capital per hour of labor.
  • Population does not explode as in Classical theory: higher income can reduce birth rates while also reducing death rates, producing offsetting forces.

Major limitation emphasized by the professor

Technology is effectively treated as arriving from outside the model / by chance. The theory therefore does not explain what determines technological change, giving policymakers little direct explanation of how to create it.

Lecture 3 • Theory 3 • p.23–26

New Growth Theory — Choices, Profit, Knowledge, and Technology

Growth is driven by choices people make in pursuit of self-interest / profit; technological progress is not merely luck.

Five market-economy facts emphasized

  1. Human capital grows because of choices — people choose education and training.
  2. Discoveries result from choices — technology depends on how many people search and how hard they try.
  3. Discoveries create profit; competition destroys profit — the loss of profit pushes firms to search for better methods and lower costs.
  4. Physical capital can be replicated — productive methods/capital can spread.
  5. Many people can use technology at the same time — knowledge has spillover/non-rival characteristics, helping avoid the simple diminishing-return story.

Link back to Lecture 1

The professor explicitly connects this theory to economic agents: households choose to build human capital; firms pursue profit and therefore have incentives to develop/adopt technology that raises productivity and growth.

Important nuance: the handwritten note “competition is bad here” is about competition eroding an innovator’s profit; that erosion itself becomes an incentive to keep innovating. It is not a claim that competition is generally undesirable.

Conditions and Policies for Sustained Economic Growth

Preconditions in the lecture

  • Economic freedom: ability to make personal/economic choices; fewer barriers from corruption, illegal markets, excessive trade barriers, taxes or restrictive regulation.
  • Property rights: protection of physical, financial and intellectual property provides incentives to work and save.
  • Markets: exchange information, products and services and enable trade, saving and investment.

What must increase?

  • Growth rate of capital per hour of labor.
  • Growth rate of human capital.
  • Pace of technological change.

Policies listed by the professor

  • Create incentives to save, invest and innovate.
  • Encourage saving.
  • Encourage research and development.
  • Use international trade.
  • Improve the quality of education.
  • Expect growth to be slow and steady, with large cumulative benefits over time.

Lecture 4 • Foundations • p.3–10

Financial Flows, Finance vs Money, Capital, Investment, Wealth, and Saving

Why finance appears in the circular flow

The lecture starts from the real economy: households, firms, government and the rest of the world have income and expenditure flows. Financial markets allow units with surpluses to provide funds to units with deficits, helping make investment and expenditure possible.

Finance vs Money

  • Finance: activity of providing funds that finance expenditure on capital; studies how households/firms obtain and use financial resources and manage the associated risks.
  • Money: what is used to pay for goods/services, factors of production and financial transactions — a medium used in transactions.

Physical vs Financial Capital

  • Physical capital: produced tools, machines, buildings and constructions used to produce goods/services.
  • Financial capital: funds firms use to buy and operate physical capital.

Gross vs Net Investment

Net Investment = Gross Investment − Depreciation

Gross investment is total spending on new capital goods; net investment is the actual change in the capital stock after replacing depreciated capital.

Wealth vs Saving

  • Wealth: value of all things a person owns.
  • Saving: income not paid in taxes or spent on consumption; saving adds to wealth and becomes a source of funds for investment.
Flow idea: คน/หน่วยที่ Income > Expenditure = saver/lender; Income < Expenditure = borrower/spender.
Lecture 4 • Financial Markets & Institutions • p.11–19

Loans, Bonds, Stocks, Financial Institutions, and Asset Prices

Three markets for financial capital

  • Loan markets: bank/credit loans used by firms for inventories/credit and by households for large purchases.
  • Bond markets: a bond is a promise by the issuer to pay specified sums on specified dates; it is debt for the issuer. Firms and governments issue bonds.
  • Stock markets: stock is a certificate of ownership and a claim on the profits of a firm.

Direct vs indirect finance

The professor’s flow diagram distinguishes direct movement of funds through financial markets from indirect finance through financial intermediaries.

Financial institutions

A financial institution operates on both sides of financial-capital markets — it borrows in one market and lends in another. The lecture lists investment banks, commercial banks, government-sponsored mortgage lenders, pension funds and insurance companies.

Solvency, net worth, and liquidity

  • Net worth = market value of what an institution has lent / its assets minus market value of what it has borrowed / liabilities.
  • Positive net worth → solvent; negative net worth → insolvent.
  • The lecture also distinguishes a shortage of immediately usable funds / liquidity from insolvency: a firm can own valuable assets but still face difficulty meeting short-term cash needs.

Interest rates and asset prices

Other things equal: Asset price ↑ → interest rate / yield ↓   |   Asset price ↓ → interest rate / yield ↑

Stocks, bonds and loans are financial assets. The lecture frames the interest rate as a return relative to the asset’s price, producing the inverse relationship.

Lecture 4 • Loanable Funds • p.20–30

The Loanable Funds Market: Flows, Demand, Supply, and Shifts

Loanable funds market = aggregate of the markets for loans, bonds and stocks, represented with one average real interest rate.

Where loanable funds come from

  1. Private saving
  2. Government budget surplus
  3. International borrowing

What loanable funds are used for

  1. Business investment
  2. Government budget deficit
  3. International investment / lending

Demand for loanable funds (DLF)

  • Major private use = financing investment.
  • Investment depends on the real interest rate and expected profit.
  • Real interest rate ↑ → quantity of loanable funds demanded ↓; real interest rate ↓ → quantity demanded ↑.
  • Expected profit ↑ → investment ↑ → DLF shifts right. Expected profit ↓ → DLF shifts left.

Supply of loanable funds (SLF)

  • Main private source = saving.
  • Saving / supply is influenced by: real interest rate, disposable income, wealth, expected future income, and default risk.
  • Other things equal: real interest rate ↑ → saving and quantity of loanable funds supplied ↑.
กราฟ: r คือ “price/return” ของ loanable funds. DLF ลงลาด; SLF ขึ้นลาด.
Lecture 4 • Equilibrium • p.31–34

Loanable Funds Equilibrium and Market Adjustment

Equilibrium: Quantity of loanable funds supplied = Quantity demanded.
  • If the real interest rate is above equilibrium → quantity supplied > quantity demanded → surplus of funds → real interest rate falls.
  • If the real interest rate is below equilibrium → quantity demanded > quantity supplied → shortage of funds → real interest rate rises.
Lecture 4 • Government • p.35–42

Government in the Loanable Funds Market: Deficit, Crowding Out, and Ricardo–Barro

Government budget position

  • A budget surplus is a source of loanable funds in the lecture’s flow framework.
  • A budget deficit is a use/demand for loanable funds and is added to private demand.

Deficit mechanism emphasized in the lecture

Budget deficit ↑ → DLF right → real interest rate ↑ → private investment ↓
  • The higher real interest rate induces a greater quantity of private funds supplied.
  • But the higher borrowing cost reduces firms’ investment and private quantity of loanable funds demanded.

Crowding-out effect

Crowding out is the tendency for a government budget deficit to raise the real interest rate and reduce private investment.

Ricardo–Barro effect

The Ricardo–Barro proposition says a budget deficit need not change the real interest rate or investment if private saving rises enough to offset the deficit. Then SLF shifts right by the amount of the deficit and the real interest rate does not change. The lecture explicitly notes that most economists regard this complete offset as unlikely.

Graph trap: the standard deficit story in this lecture is a rightward shift of demand for loanable funds, not a movement along the same DLF curve.
